## Current Research Focus

> **How does coverage of five predefined workflow exceptions differ between Product Requirement Prompts created through user-led context provision and agent-led questioning for a fixed expense-approval scenario?**

The study compares two context-gathering processes and measures one result: correct coverage of five known workflow exceptions in the final PRP.

## 1. Proposal and Research Paper: Content, Purpose, and Selection

A proposal and a completed research paper do not represent two writing styles from which the researcher freely chooses. They represent different stages of research:

- choose a **proposal** when asking for approval, assessment, access, ethics clearance, time, or resources to conduct a future study;
- write a **research paper** after conducting the study, when reporting evidence and stating what the findings support.

The sections overlap because both must present a sound research argument. Their contents differ because a proposal contains justified plans, while a paper contains records and evidence from completed work.

| Part | Contents in a proposal | Why it belongs in a proposal | Contents in a completed paper | Why it belongs in a paper |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Title | Working topic, setting, main comparison, and outcome | Sets boundaries before the study starts | Final title that reflects the study actually completed | Prevents the title from claiming more than the evidence supports |
| Abstract or summary | Problem, gap, question, proposed method, and expected contribution | Lets a reviewer judge the whole plan quickly | Problem, method, main findings, and actual contribution | Lets a reader judge the completed study quickly |
| Background and problem | Practical setting, affected people, problem, importance, and proposed scope | Shows why the study deserves attention | Final problem framing informed by the completed research | Places the findings within the problem that the study addressed |
| Literature review | Initial synthesis of what is known, disputed, and missing | Establishes the gap and supports the planned question | Fuller synthesis linked to the findings and contribution | Shows how the findings confirm, change, or challenge prior work |
| Theory or framework | Chosen concepts and expected role in the study | Shows how theory will guide the question, data, or analysis | Theory used in the study and what the findings mean for it | Links the evidence back to established knowledge |
| Research gap | A precise claim about missing knowledge | Gives the proposed study a reason to exist | The gap addressed by the completed evidence | Shows exactly what part of the gap the paper did and did not address |
| Aim, questions, or hypotheses | What the researcher plans to answer or test | Controls the scope and determines the method | Final questions or hypotheses, including any justified changes | Gives the results and discussion a clear target |
| Methodology | Research logic, assumptions, design, and reason for choosing it | Shows that the planned approach fits the question | The approach actually used and any departure from the plan | Allows readers to judge whether the evidence supports the claims |
| Methods | Planned participants, sampling, task, data, measures, procedure, and analysis | Shows that the study is feasible and could be repeated | What the researcher actually did | Makes the study open to review and possible replication |
| Ethics and data care | Expected risks, consent, privacy, storage, and safeguards | Protects participants before data collection starts | Approval, consent, safeguards used, and any issues encountered | Shows that the completed study met its duties |
| Expected contribution | The kind of knowledge the study may produce | Shows value without pretending to know the result | Not used as a substitute for findings | A completed paper must support its contribution with evidence |
| Results | Not included, except pilot evidence clearly labelled as such | The main study has not occurred | Analysed observations, scores, themes, or other findings | Reports what the evidence shows before interpretation |
| Discussion | Expected implications may be stated cautiously, but not as findings | Avoids claiming an answer before collecting evidence | Meaning of results, relation to theory and literature, alternative explanations, and implications | Turns results into a supported research contribution |
| Limits | Foreseeable scope limits, risks, access problems, and threats to validity | Shows realistic planning | Limits observed during the study and their effect on the claims | Prevents claims from extending beyond the evidence |
| Feasibility, timeline, or budget | Tasks, access, resources, and schedule where required | Shows that the proposed work can be completed | Usually omitted unless the paper studies the research process itself | These planning details no longer form the main scholarly claim |
| Conclusion | Short statement of the proposed study and its value, if required | Leaves the reviewer with a clear case for approving the work | Answer to the question, contribution, limits, and future work | Closes the argument using completed evidence |
| References | Every source cited in the plan | Shows the proposal's knowledge base | Every source cited in the completed paper | Makes the final argument traceable |

### Why Some Sections Are Selected and Others Are Not

Section choice should follow the research question, contribution, method, audience, and assessment brief.

- Add **hypotheses** when theory supports a clear prediction that the study will test. Do not add them only to make an exploratory study appear more formal.
- Add a distinct **artefact and evaluation** section when the study develops a method, tool, model, or design principle, such as a Gregor Type V study.
- Add **reflexivity** when the researcher's role, position, or interpretation materially shapes qualitative data and analysis.
- Add a detailed **sampling and measurement** section for a quantitative comparison because readers must judge group selection, variables, and scoring.
- Add a detailed **data-analysis** section for qualitative research because readers must understand how codes, themes, and interpretations will be produced.
- Add **results and discussion** only when reporting completed research. A proposal may state possible outcomes but cannot present them as facts.
- Add a **timeline, access plan, or budget** when the audience must judge feasibility or allocate resources.

### What This Means for the PRP Study

The current proposal should emphasise:

- the problem of missing workflow exceptions;
- prior work on AI-led requirements elicitation and requirements coverage;
- Robson and Robinson's information-seeking and communication model;
- the precise comparison question;
- the fixed scenario and five predefined exceptions;
- how the two context-gathering conditions differ;
- the scoring guide and analysis;
- ethics for AI chat records; and
- the expected Type I contribution.

It should not include a results section. It should include hypotheses only if the study will predict and test that agent-led questioning achieves higher coverage. It needs a separate design-artefact section only if the paper's main contribution becomes the design of the questioning protocol rather than the comparison of coverage.

## 2. Who Is the Audience, and What Theory Is Useful?

### Likely Audience

Until the official brief says otherwise, write for an academic assessor who understands IS research but does not know the proposed PRP study in detail. The assessor needs to see that:

- the problem matters;
- the gap follows from the literature;
- the question is precise;
- the theory helps with the question;
- the method can answer the question;
- the measures have clear meanings;
- the study is ethical and feasible; and
- the claimed contribution matches the evidence the study could produce.

The proposal should therefore focus on the research logic, not on selling an AI product or explaining every technical detail of coding agents.

### Most Useful Course Theory

Robson and Robinson's (2013) **Information Seeking and Communication Model** is the closest substantive theory from the course readings.

It brings together:

- an information seeker;
- an information provider;
- an information need or knowledge gap;
- personal and situational factors;
- decisions about seeking and communicating information; and
- whether the information is received and used successfully.

This fits the study because the two conditions organise the information exchange differently:

| Condition | Information process |
| --- | --- |
| User-led context provision | The user decides what to communicate without AI follow-up questions |
| Agent-led questioning | The agent identifies a knowledge gap and asks the user for the missing context |

The model may help explain why one process exposes workflow exceptions that the other misses. Because the original model did not focus on AI-led PRP creation, the proposal must explain and justify its use in this new setting rather than assume it transfers without change.

### Role of Gregor and Research through Design

- **Gregor (2006)** classifies the kind of theory contribution. It does not by itself explain how the human and agent exchange information.
- The current comparison question chiefly supports **Type I analysis** if it describes differences in exception coverage.
- It may support **Type IV explanation and prediction** if the proposal states and tests a predicted effect under controlled conditions.
- **Research through Design** and Gregor **Type V** become central only if the main aim is to design, test, and refine the agent-questioning protocol and state reusable design principles.

For a small paper, use one main theory role. The Information Seeking and Communication Model can guide the analysis; Gregor can state what kind of contribution the study aims to make.

## 3. How Much Initial Literature Review Is Needed?

The initial review is sufficient when it can support every step in this argument:

1. AI assistants are being used to turn business context into software requirements or prompts.
2. Missing workflow exceptions can weaken requirements and later implementation.
3. User-led and agent-led context gathering differ in how information gaps get exposed.
4. Prior research has not adequately compared exception coverage under the proposed conditions.
5. The chosen theory helps organise or explain that comparison.
6. The chosen method and measure have support in prior research.

### Literature Streams for This Proposal

Keep the search narrow:

1. generative AI or conversational agents in requirements elicitation;
2. ambiguity, completeness, and exception handling in software requirements;
3. information seeking and communication between a requester and provider;
4. user-led versus system-led clarification dialogue; and
5. methods for assessing requirement or PRP coverage.

The review should include:

- key foundational sources for the theory and requirements concepts;
- recent peer-reviewed studies closest to AI-led elicitation;
- studies with similar methods or measures; and
- sources that show limits, risks, or conflicting findings.

There is no sound universal number of papers. Unless the assessment brief sets a minimum, judge sufficiency by coverage and synthesis rather than count. Stop expanding the initial review when new searches mostly repeat known findings and each part of the proposal's argument has sound support.

Levy and Ellis's (2006) input–processing–output approach is useful here. Do not only collect sources. Compare their concepts, methods, findings, limits, and relevance to the proposed question. A literature matrix can record these points.

## 4. How Do You Choose the Methodology?

Start from the research question and intended claim, not from a preferred method.

The current question asks whether **coverage differs between two conditions**. A small controlled comparative study fits that question better than interviews alone.

### Proposed Study Design

| Element | Proposed choice | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design | Exploratory controlled comparison | Compares two context-gathering conditions while keeping other factors stable |
| Scenario | One expense-approval feature | Keeps the task and domain fixed |
| Conditions | User-led context provision and agent-led questioning | Represents the independent variable |
| AI setup | Same model, instructions, PRP template, and access | Reduces avoidable variation |
| Ground truth | Five predefined workflow exceptions | Gives a clear basis for scoring coverage |
| Primary data | AI interaction record and final PRP | Shows both the process and result |
| Primary measure | Exception-coverage score from 0 to 10 | Directly answers the question |
| Quality safeguard | Blind scoring by more than one rater where feasible | Reduces bias and allows agreement checking |
| Supporting analysis | Review which exceptions and question types were most often missed | Helps interpret the coverage scores |

### Conditions

- **User-led:** The participant chooses which available business facts to give the AI. The AI creates the PRP without asking follow-up questions.
- **Agent-led:** The participant starts with the same business brief. The AI asks focused questions before creating the PRP.

Both conditions must have access to the same business facts. Otherwise, the study would compare access to knowledge rather than the context-gathering process.

### Scoring

Score each predefined workflow exception:

- `0`: missing or wrong;
- `1`: present but unclear or incomplete; and
- `2`: correct and clear.

The total score ranges from 0 to 10. Define each score before data collection and give raters the same guide.

### Analysis

For a small exploratory study:

- report the scores and their spread for each condition;
- compare which exceptions each condition captured or missed;
- analyse the agent questions that produced useful new context; and
- avoid broad claims beyond the participants, scenario, model, and task used.

Use a formal test of statistical significance only if the sample and design can support it. Otherwise, describe the study as exploratory and use the comparison to identify patterns rather than claim a general causal effect.

### Ethics and Feasibility

- obtain informed consent;
- do not collect confidential workplace requirements;
- use a fictional or safely altered scenario;
- remove names and other identifiers from chat records;
- state how transcripts will be stored and deleted;
- tell participants that an AI system is involved; and
- avoid judging participants' competence from what their PRP contains.

## Discussion Answers

### One-Minute Answer

> A proposal and a completed paper share a problem, literature review, theory, question, method, and references, but a proposal justifies work that has not yet been done. Its audience needs to see a clear, feasible link from the gap to the question and method. For my topic, Robson and Robinson's Information Seeking and Communication Model can help explain the exchange between the user and AI, while Gregor helps classify the intended contribution. The initial literature review is sufficient when it defines the concepts, establishes the gap, supports the theory and method, and new searches mostly repeat known findings. Because my question compares exception coverage in two controlled conditions, a small comparative study using one scenario, five predefined exceptions, the same AI setup, and a clear scoring guide would fit better than interviews alone.

## Questions to Raise in the Tutorial

- Does the proposal brief require a set structure or source count, or can we organise sections around the research logic?
- Is Gregor expected as the study's theory, or can it classify a contribution while another model guides the analysis?
- For a small exploratory comparison, what level of sample-size and statistical justification does the subject expect?
- Can a checklist act as the conceptual measure of completeness, or must it come from an existing tested instrument?
- How much method detail is expected when the assessment asks for a proposal rather than a full ethics application?
