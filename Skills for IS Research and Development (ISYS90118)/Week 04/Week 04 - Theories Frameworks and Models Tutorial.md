## Key Distinctions

Researchers do not always use these terms in the same way. The label matters less than the role the idea plays in the study.

| Term | Main role | Useful question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Theory | Gives linked ideas that analyse, explain, predict, or guide action | What does this help the researcher understand, explain, predict, or do? |
| Framework | Organises concepts and may show how they relate | What parts of the issue should the researcher examine? |
| Model | Gives a simpler account of a system, process, or set of links | What does the model represent, and what does it leave out? |

Levy and Ellis (2006) note that IS research often uses *model* and *theoretical framework* for a linked set of concepts or variables. This overlap is one reason to inspect a work's purpose and claims instead of relying on its label.

## Gregor's Five Types of Theory

Gregor (2006) sorts IS theory by four main goals: analysis, explanation, prediction, and prescription. Her five types show that a sound theory does not need to do every job.

| Type | Main purpose | Core question | Typical output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I. Analysis | Describes and sorts what exists | What is it? | Concepts, categories, taxonomies, or ways to classify events |
| II. Explanation | Explains how, why, when, or where events occur | How or why did it happen? | An account of causes, reasons, or processes without a strong focus on testable prediction |
| III. Prediction | Predicts outcomes without a full causal account | What will happen? | Testable predictions about links or outcomes |
| IV. Explanation and prediction | Explains causes and predicts outcomes | What will happen, and why? | Causal claims, linked constructs, propositions, and testable hypotheses |
| V. Design and action | Tells people how to build or act | How can this be done? | Design rules, methods, principles, or a working artefact |

### Why the Types Matter

Gregor's classification helps a researcher:

- match the research question to the intended type of contribution;
- choose methods and evidence that can support that contribution;
- judge a study by the right standard;
- avoid claiming explanation when the study only describes or predicts;
- spot how one body of work could join several theory types; and
- state what the research adds to knowledge.

For example, a list of barriers to system use may offer analysis. A study of why those barriers arise may offer explanation. A survey that forecasts use may offer prediction. A tested account of why certain factors lead to use may offer explanation and prediction. A set of tested interface rules may offer design and action.

## Draft Answers to the Tutorial Questions

### 1. Why is it important to understand different types of theory, framework, or model?

Gregor shows that research can aim to analyse, explain, predict, or guide action. Knowing the intended aim helps us judge whether the research question, method, evidence, and conclusion fit each other. It also stops us from treating every theory as if it must predict outcomes. A framework that sorts concepts can make a valid contribution even when it does not explain causes. The key test is whether the work does the job it claims to do.

### 2. Did the earlier articles use, develop, or expand theories?

This must be answered from the chosen cluster articles. Use these tests:

| Relationship to theory | Signs in the article |
| --- | --- |
| Uses | Applies an existing theory to choose constructs, form questions or hypotheses, guide data collection, or explain results |
| Develops | Builds a new set of concepts, links, causes, propositions, or design rules from evidence |
| Expands | Adds a construct, link, boundary condition, level of analysis, setting, or process to an existing theory |
| Tests | Checks whether stated links or predictions hold in the chosen data and setting |
| Challenges | Finds results or cases that conflict with a theory and argues that it needs change |

Do not rely only on the article's theory section. Check how the authors use the theory in the research question, method, analysis, findings, and claimed contribution.

### 3. Why should research include a theory, framework, or model?

It gives the study a clear lens and makes the reasoning open to review. It can define the key concepts, show which links matter, guide the research question and method, help the researcher explain the findings, and show how the study adds to what is known. Levy and Ellis (2006) also argue that a sound literature review should build the study's theoretical base, show the research gap, support the method, and explain the new contribution. A theory should guide the study, not serve as a name added after the analysis.

### 4. How can theories inform research at different levels?

The level of analysis should match what the theory explains and what the data can support.

| Research context | Possible focus | Check before applying a theory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individual | Use, trust, skill, choice, belief, or behaviour | Does the theory explain people rather than teams or firms? |
| Group | Team work, shared knowledge, power, or coordination | Does it include group processes and shared outcomes? |
| Organisational | Strategy, culture, structure, change, or system uptake | Can it explain firm-level choices and effects? |
| Inter-organisational | Partners, platforms, supply chains, or data sharing | Does it cover dependence and links between organisations? |
| Community and society | Access, fairness, public value, norms, or social effects | Whose views and outcomes does the theory include or exclude? |
| Cross-border | Culture, law, global work, or transfer between nations | Do its assumptions hold across places and groups? |

A common error is a level mismatch. Data from people do not by themselves prove a claim about an entire organisation or society.

## Set-Reading Discussion Points

These points provide a fallback if the discussion turns to the readings listed on the Canvas page. They do not replace the two chosen cluster theories.

### Gregor (2006): A Classification of IS Theory

- The work develops a classification of IS theories based on their purpose.
- In Gregor's own terms, the classification chiefly supports analysis because it defines and sorts theory types.
- It also supports research design by helping researchers align questions, methods, and claims.
- A useful discussion point is that IS needs several valid kinds of theory, including theory for design and action.

### Levy and Ellis (2006): Literature Review Framework

- The article develops an input–processing–output framework for literature reviews.
- Inputs cover finding and screening sound literature.
- Processing covers knowing, understanding, applying, analysing, bringing together, and judging the literature.
- Outputs cover writing the review and building an argument for the study.
- The framework supports a study by linking the body of knowledge to its theoretical base, research gap, questions, method, and claimed contribution.

### Lee, Mitchell, and Sablynski (1999): Qualitative Research and Theory

- The article reviews qualitative research in organisational and work psychology.
- It presents qualitative methods as useful for some types of research question and discusses their role in theory generation, theory elaboration, and theory testing.
- This supports the point that method choice should follow the research question and intended theory contribution.
- It also offers a link to organisational research contexts, where processes, meanings, and lived work settings may need close study.

The Canvas rubric gives the top range for leading some discussion and asking questions. Aim to make useful links rather than speaking often without adding detail.

### Opening Comment

> Gregor's main point is that theory can serve different purposes. Before we judge a paper's theory, we should ask whether it aims to analyse, explain, predict, or guide action. That choice should shape its question, method, evidence, and claim.

### Comments to Prepare

1. Explain one chosen theory and give a research question it could guide.
2. Compare the two chosen theories by their purpose or level of analysis.
3. Link a classmate's example to one of Gregor's five types.
4. Point out one limit, missing view, or level mismatch.

### Questions to Ask

- If a framework sorts concepts but does not explain causes, should we judge it as incomplete or as a valid Type I contribution?
- Can one study make more than one type of theory contribution? What evidence would each claim need?
- How can we tell whether an article truly expands a theory rather than only applying it in a new setting?
- What can go wrong when a theory created for individuals gets used to explain an organisation or society?
- When does a model simplify a problem so much that it stops helping the research?
