## Cluster Purpose

Cluster 7 examines two linked questions:

1. What kinds of new knowledge can human–computer interaction (HCI) research contribute?
2. How can designing and making interactive artefacts become part of the research process?

The first paper classifies research contributions. The second explains Research through Design (RtD), where design work helps generate knowledge.

## Foundation: What Design Means in Research

### Design Is an Activity, Not Just Appearance

In research, *design* usually means the purposeful activity of creating or changing something for a stated setting and need. It includes:

- deciding what problem to address;
- understanding the people and setting involved;
- exploring several possible responses;
- making choices under limits and competing needs;
- creating sketches, models, prototypes, systems, services, or processes;
- testing what those creations make possible; and
- revising the problem and response as new facts emerge.

Design therefore includes judgement, framing, making, testing, and change. It does not only mean an interface's colours, layout, or visual style.

### Design and Research Have Different Usual Goals

| Point | Design practice | Research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main aim | Create a suitable response to a specific situation | Produce knowledge that others can inspect and use |
| Typical result | Product, service, process, system, or experience | Finding, explanation, theory, method, dataset, or other knowledge claim |
| Main setting | Here and now | May apply beyond the original case, within stated limits |
| Main test | Does it serve its intended purpose? | Is the claim new, sound, supported, and clear about its scope? |

The two activities can overlap. Design work uses research when it studies people, technology, and the setting. Research uses design when it creates prototypes, study tools, interventions, or new possible futures.

### Three Ways Design Can Relate to Research

1. **Design as the subject:** Researchers study how design happens. This is research *into* design.
2. **Research supporting a design:** Researchers gather facts to improve a specific response. This is research *for* design.
3. **Design as a way of finding out:** Researchers make and revise artefacts to generate knowledge. This is research *through* design.

### When Does Design Become Research?

Design becomes part of research when the project moves from only making a response to making a supported knowledge claim. It needs:

- a clear research question;
- links to what others already know;
- planned and recorded design choices and iterations;
- evidence from making, use, tests, or reflection;
- a clear account of what the work revealed;
- a claim that others can assess; and
- stated limits on where the claim may apply.

A useful artefact is not enough by itself. The researcher must explain what was learned through creating or using it.

### Four Parts to Keep Separate

| Part | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Artefact | What did we create? |
| Design rationale | Why did we make these choices? |
| Evidence | What happened when we made, tested, or used it? |
| Research contribution | What can others learn or reuse from this work? |

### Simple Example

Design-practice result:

> We created a new university timetable app that students found useful.

Research result:

> By comparing three ways of displaying timetable clashes, we found that showing the clash beside the affected choice helped students correct plans with fewer missed constraints. We state the result as a design principle for systems that support linked scheduling choices.

The second statement moves beyond the app. It names the design comparison, evidence, finding, and reusable knowledge.

### Link to Gregor

Design research often produces **Gregor Type V: theory for design and action**. Its output may include principles, methods, or rules about how to create an artefact or bring about a result. Design work can also support other theory types when it produces classifications, explanations, or tested predictions.

## HCI in Simple Terms

HCI studies relations between people and interactive technology. It covers more than interface appearance or ease of use. It can study:

- how people understand and use systems;
- how systems affect behaviour, work, access, and social life;
- new ways to interact with technology;
- methods for studying or designing interaction;
- concepts and theories that explain interaction; and
- possible future systems and the questions they raise.

## Wobbrock and Kientz: Seven HCI Contribution Types

### Main Point

Every research paper should state what new knowledge it contributes. HCI is broad, so valid contributions take several forms. Each type needs its own test of quality.

| Contribution | What it adds | How it is judged | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Empirical | Findings based on observed or collected data | Importance of findings and soundness of the method | User test, interview study, experiment, field observation, diary, survey, or system log analysis |
| Artefact | A new system, tool, technique, design, sketch, or view of a possible future | What it makes possible, how well it works for its purpose, and how well it resolves design trade-offs | Accessible input method, prototype, design toolkit, or new interface |
| Methodological | A new or improved way to research or design | Usefulness, repeatability, reliability, and validity | New usability measure, interview method, design process, or analysis method |
| Theoretical | A new or improved concept, definition, model, principle, or framework | Novelty, soundness, ability to describe, explain, or predict, and ability to be tested | Model of trust in AI interaction or framework for technology abandonment |
| Dataset | A useful new set of data for other researchers | Quality, coverage, description, representativeness, and value for later work | Gesture dataset, interaction log set, or accessible-interface benchmark |
| Survey | A review and synthesis that reveals patterns, trends, and gaps | Coverage, depth, maturity, structure, and value of the synthesis | Review of older adults' social-media use |
| Opinion | A reasoned case intended to persuade and start debate | Strength of argument, quality of evidence, fair treatment of other views, and wider relevance | Argument that a common usability practice causes harm in some settings |

### Do Not Confuse Contribution with Method

A contribution states what the research adds. A method states how the researcher produced or checked it.

- Interviews are a method. The interview findings may form an empirical contribution.
- A prototype is an artefact. A study of its use may also form an empirical contribution.
- A literature review is a method for synthesis. The resulting map of trends and gaps may form a survey contribution.
- A new questionnaire may form a methodological contribution, while the data collected with it may support an empirical contribution.

One paper can make several contributions. A strong HCI project might provide an artefact, an empirical finding about its use, and a design principle derived from the study.

### Link to Gregor

Wobbrock and Kientz's seven-part classification is itself closest to **Gregor Type I: theory for analysis**. It defines and sorts forms of research contribution.

The seven HCI types do not map one-to-one to Gregor's five theory types:

| HCI contribution | Possible Gregor link |
| --- | --- |
| Empirical | Can support analysis, explanation, prediction, or explanation and prediction |
| Artefact | Often supports design and action |
| Methodological | Often supports design and action by guiding research or design work |
| Theoretical | May fit any of Gregor's five types, depending on its purpose |
| Dataset | Supplies evidence for later theory but is not automatically a theory |
| Survey | Often supports analysis and may support explanation |
| Opinion | May guide later theory or action but is not automatically a theory |

Wobbrock and Kientz use a narrower view when they discuss a fully developed scientific theory: it should explain rather than only observe and should allow testing. Gregor uses a broader IS classification that also accepts analysis and design-and-action as valid theory purposes.

### Thirty-Second Tutorial Answer

> Wobbrock and Kientz identify seven HCI contribution types: empirical, artefact, methodological, theoretical, dataset, survey, and opinion. Their main point is that HCI can produce knowledge in several valid forms and that each form needs a suitable test of quality. The classification itself fits Gregor's Type I because it sorts forms of contribution. It also reminds us that a method, such as an interview, is not the same as the contribution produced from it.

## Stappers and Giaccardi: Research through Design

### Three Relations Between Research and Design

| Relation | Main purpose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Research into design | Studies design as the subject | Study of how design teams make choices |
| Research for design | Produces facts needed for a specific design | User interviews used to design a banking app |
| Research through Design | Uses designing and making as a central way to generate knowledge | Iterative prototypes used to discover and explain new interaction principles |

Research for design aims to improve a specific solution. Research through Design must also produce knowledge that others can use beyond that one solution.

### How Research through Design Produces Knowledge

RtD gives design actions a central role in inquiry. The researcher:

1. starts with a question, setting, and prior knowledge;
2. frames and reframes the problem;
3. makes sketches, prototypes, or other artefacts;
4. tests ideas against technical limits, theory, users, and real settings;
5. reflects on what each design choice reveals;
6. changes the design and repeats the process; and
7. extracts and shares the resulting knowledge through clear concepts, principles, methods, evidence, and documentation.

The act of making creates useful tension between an idea and what can work in practice. This can expose hidden needs, values, assumptions, trade-offs, and new questions.

### Roles of a Prototype

In RtD, a prototype can:

- remain open for testing and change;
- let people experience a possible future;
- connect an abstract idea with direct experience;
- support discussion between fields and stakeholders;
- create activities and prompt stories;
- record an important stage in the research; and
- shape the next research question or design direction.

A prototype can hold knowledge, but the prototype alone does not explain that knowledge. Researchers must record decisions, failed attempts, observations, trade-offs, and insights, then state what others can reuse.

### Research through Design Is More Than Product Development

Normal product development asks whether a design works in its intended setting. RtD also asks what the process teaches the wider research community.

Weak claim:

> We made a useful prototype.

Stronger research claim:

> Through several prototypes and user sessions, we found that showing an AI system's uncertainty before users act helps them check high-risk suggestions. We express this finding as a design principle for later systems.

The second claim identifies knowledge that may apply beyond the single prototype.

### Link to Gregor

RtD often supports **Gregor Type V: design and action** because it can produce design principles, methods, and artefacts. It can also produce:

- Type I concepts or classifications;
- Type II explanations of an experience or design process;
- Type III predictions that later studies can test; or
- Type IV explanations and predictions supported by later empirical work.

The research question and stated contribution determine the Gregor type. The fact that a project includes a prototype does not settle the classification.

### Thirty-Second Tutorial Answer

> Research through Design uses designing and prototyping as part of generating knowledge, not only as a way to make a product. Each iteration can expose needs, assumptions, trade-offs, and new interaction principles. The key step is to capture those insights and explain what others can reuse beyond the prototype. It often fits Gregor's Type V because it produces design knowledge and guidance, but it can also produce concepts or explanations.

| RtD output | HCI contribution type |
| --- | --- |
| Working prototype | Artefact |
| Findings from people using it | Empirical |

| Argument for a different design direction | Opinion |

## Worked Example: AI Study Planner

### Topic

Design an AI study planner that gives useful advice without hiding uncertainty or weakening student control.

### Research Question

> How can an AI study planner show uncertainty in ways that help students check advice and retain control over study decisions?

### Research through Design Process

1. Interview students about planning, trust, mistakes, and current tools.
2. Create several ways to show uncertain advice.
3. Let students use the prototypes for realistic planning tasks.
4. Record where they question, accept, change, or ignore the advice.
5. Revise the prototypes based on observed problems and stated needs.
6. Compare the design options and identify repeatable design principles.
7. State where those principles may or may not apply.

### Possible Contributions

- **Artefact:** An interactive AI-planning prototype.
- **Empirical:** Findings about how students interpret uncertainty cues.
- **Theoretical:** A model linking uncertainty display, checking behaviour, and user control.
- **Methodological:** A task for testing how users respond to uncertain AI advice.
- **Gregor Type V:** Design principles for showing uncertain advice.
- **Gregor Type II or IV:** An account of why some uncertainty displays encourage checking, with later prediction if evidence supports it.

## Critical Points for Discussion

### 1. A New Interface Is Not Enough

Novelty alone does not establish a research contribution. Researchers must show what the artefact makes possible or what knowledge its creation and use produced.

### 2. Evaluation Must Match the Claim

- A speed claim needs a suitable performance test.
- A lived-experience claim may need interviews or field work.
- A design-method claim needs evidence of usefulness and repeatability.
- A theoretical claim needs a clear account that later evidence could test.

### 3. Negative Results Can Still Produce Knowledge

A failed prototype can expose a false assumption, a design limit, an unmet need, or a harmful trade-off. It becomes useful research when the researcher records and explains the lesson.

### 4. Knowledge Must Leave the Artefact

If insights remain hidden in the prototype or only in the designer's experience, others cannot inspect or reuse them. RtD needs clear records and a stated contribution.

### 5. HCI Contributions Can Conflict

A visually rich prototype might prompt valuable debate but perform poorly in a speed test. That does not make it worthless. It may offer a strong artefact or opinion contribution while failing a performance claim. The researcher must state the intended contribution clearly.

## One-Minute Tutorial Answer

> Cluster 7 shows that design can contribute to research in several ways. Wobbrock and Kientz identify seven HCI contribution types: empirical, artefact, methodological, theoretical, dataset, survey, and opinion. This helps us judge work by what it claims to add. Stappers and Giaccardi then explain Research through Design, where making and testing prototypes helps generate knowledge. The important point is that building an artefact is not enough. Researchers must capture what the design process revealed and state knowledge that others can inspect or reuse. The Wobbrock and Kientz classification fits Gregor Type I, while Research through Design often supports Type V design-and-action theory.
