# PISC 6Cs Exam Study Guide

Course: [[Professional IS Consulting (ISYS90045)/Course Overview|Professional IS Consulting]]  
Framework: **Client -> Commit -> Communicate -> Consult -> Control -> Close**  
Use: organise PISC exam study around the way a consulting engagement moves from client need to final sign-off.

## Core Memory Line

> A consultant understands the **Client**, **Commits** through a clear proposal and agreement, **Communicates** advice, **Consults** through evidence and problem solving, **Controls** risk and conflict, then **Closes** with sign-off and handover.

This does not replace the weekly lecture order. It gives a simpler mental map for scenario questions, where the task is usually to identify what part of the consulting engagement is under pressure and explain the professional response.

| 6C section | Plain meaning | Main course links |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Client | Who is the client, why are consultants involved, and what trust or stakeholder issue matters? | Consulting role, audience, trust, stakeholder context |
| Commit | What work is being proposed, agreed, priced, scoped, and negotiated? | Proposals, engagement life cycle, contracts, negotiation |
| Communicate | How is the consultant making evidence, advice, reports, and presentations usable for the client? | Professional communication, advice, report writing |
| Consult | How is the consultant gathering evidence, framing problems, testing hypotheses, and solving the issue? | Meetings, interviews, workshops, hypothesis-driven consulting, problem solving |
| Control | What risk, governance, scope, conflict, or difficult-stakeholder issue needs active management? | Risk, governance, scope creep, conflict, escalation |
| Close | How is the engagement completed, accepted, handed over, and protected after delivery? | Close-out, sign-off, residual risk, lessons learned |

## What the 6Cs Fix

The PISC exam rewards applied consulting judgement, not definition dumping. The 6Cs stop common weak answers:

1. Do not treat the client as a generic organisation; identify the sponsor, audience, stakeholders, trust issue, and context.
2. Do not treat the proposal as a sales document only; it sets scope, method, price, assumptions, and delivery risk.
3. Do not present information as if it is advice; advice needs analysis, judgement, recommendation, limits, and next steps.
4. Do not jump to a solution; gather evidence, test assumptions, and structure the problem.
5. Do not ignore scope, governance, and conflict; consulting work fails when risks are not owned or escalated.
6. Do not end at delivery; close-out needs acceptance, sign-off, handover, residual risks, and lessons learned.

## Quick Scenario Selector

| If the scenario says... | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| The organisation is unsure whether to hire consultants or what value consultants add | Client |
| The client distrusts the consultant, hides information, or reacts badly to advice | Client or Control |
| The question asks about RFPs, pricing, scope, proposal content, or bid/no-bid judgement | Commit |
| The client wants a fixed low price, fast timeline, or extra work without changing the contract | Commit or Control |
| The consultant needs to persuade executives or explain technical material to managers | Communicate |
| The deliverable is a report, presentation, finding, or recommendation | Communicate |
| The problem is messy, ambiguous, political, or poorly defined | Consult |
| The consultant must gather stakeholder evidence | Consult |
| The question asks about meetings, interviews, workshops, hypotheses, or problem-solving steps | Consult |
| The scenario includes risks, scope creep, unclear ownership, conflict, resistance, or escalation | Control |
| The work is ending, disputed, ready for handover, or needs formal acceptance | Close |

## Section 1 - Client

### What This Section Means

Client is about understanding the consulting relationship before choosing tools or giving advice. The consultant needs to know why external help is being used, who the real decision makers are, what the client values, what stakeholders fear or need, and what trust must be built before advice will be accepted.

### What to Study

| Area | Must know |
| --- | --- |
| Consulting role | Consultants bring expertise, structure, independence, credibility, temporary capacity, and professional judgement. |
| IS consulting scope | IS consulting can involve strategy, architecture, business analysis, governance, project delivery, testing, change, and stakeholder communication. |
| Why clients hire consultants | Lack of time, internal capability, specialist expertise, confidence, independence, or structured delivery methods. |
| Consulting firm types | Large management consultancies, vendor consultancies, and independent consultancies offer different value and constraints. |
| Professional conduct | Reliability, preparation, listening, tone, punctuality, client focus, and appropriate boundaries. |
| Trust | Credibility, reliability, intimacy, and low self-orientation. |
| Audience awareness | Executives, technical evaluators, procurement teams, operational staff, users, and sponsors need different information. |
| Client ecosystem | The client is not one voice; sponsor, users, business units, IT, procurement, and governance bodies may have different interests. |

### Techniques and Outputs

| Technique/task | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Stakeholder analysis | Who decides, who influences, who provides evidence, who resists, and who must act on advice. |
| Audience analysis | Message, evidence, language, detail, and format adapted to the audience. |
| Trust diagnosis | What is weakening credibility, reliability, intimacy, or low self-orientation. |
| Client-context framing | Why the client needs external support and what organisational constraints shape the engagement. |
| Professional-conduct response | Practical next action that protects trust and role clarity. |

### Exam Triggers

| If the question says... | Think Client |
| --- | --- |
| "Why would the client hire a consultant?" | Explain expertise, independence, capacity, credibility, method, and judgement. |
| "The client does not trust the consultant" | Use credibility, reliability, intimacy, and low self-orientation. |
| "The audience is senior management/non-technical/technical/procurement" | Adapt language, evidence, detail, and decision focus. |
| "The consultant is acting unprofessionally" | Discuss conduct, preparation, tone, client focus, and relationship risk. |
| "The client has many departments with different views" | Treat the client ecosystem as multiple stakeholders, not one unified opinion. |

### What a Strong Client Answer Includes

- the client's reason for using a consultant;
- the stakeholder or audience group being affected;
- the trust issue or professional role issue;
- evidence from the scenario;
- why the issue matters for information access, advice acceptance, or delivery;
- a specific professional action, such as clarifying expectations, adapting communication, meeting the sponsor, or rebuilding trust through evidence and reliability.

### Common Client Mistakes

- Saying consultants simply "fix problems".
- Treating the sponsor, users, IT, procurement, and executives as if they all want the same thing.
- Mentioning trust without explaining which part of trust is weak.
- Ignoring the commercial relationship between client and consultant.
- Writing generic stakeholder lists without explaining why each stakeholder matters.

## Section 2 - Commit

### What This Section Means

Commit is about turning a possible consulting opportunity into an agreed engagement. This includes deciding whether to bid, understanding the client request, writing a proposal, making assumptions explicit, pricing the work, negotiating the contract, and ensuring both sides understand scope and responsibilities.

### What to Study

| Area | Must know |
| --- | --- |
| Proposal purpose | A proposal explains the client problem, proposed services, approach, budget, team, assumptions, and evidence of similar work. |
| Request types | RFI, EOI, RFQ, RFT, and RFP have different levels of formality, pricing focus, and solution detail. |
| Proposal structure | Executive summary, introduction, understanding of needs, methodology and timeline, pricing and assumptions, team CVs, and similar work. |
| Executive summary vs introduction | Executive summary is decision-focused; introduction gives background, scope, and document structure. |
| Bid/no-bid judgement | Check capability, client fit, risk, time, profitability, mandatory criteria, reputation, and strategic value. |
| Engagement life cycle | Risk assessment and internal inquiry, winning the work, contractual negotiation, delivery and project management, close-out and quality assurance. |
| Contractual negotiation | Scope, budget, legal clauses, responsibilities, timelines, IP, confidentiality, and sign-off conditions. |
| Scope and assumptions | Inclusions, exclusions, dependencies, client responsibilities, and limits must be documented. |

### Techniques and Outputs

| Technique/task | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Bid/no-bid review | Decision to pursue, decline, or qualify the opportunity. |
| Proposal response | Written offer aligned to the client's need, audience, and selection criteria. |
| Methodology and timeline | Credible delivery approach, stages, milestones, responsibilities, and governance. |
| Pricing and assumptions table | Cost logic, exclusions, constraints, dependencies, and conditions. |
| Negotiation preparation | Bottom line, walk-away point, negotiable items, non-negotiables, objective criteria, and likely objections. |
| Contract clarification | Agreed scope, deliverables, sign-off, confidentiality, IP, responsibilities, and change-control path. |

### Exam Triggers

| If the question says... | Think Commit |
| --- | --- |
| "The client has issued an RFP/RFT/RFQ" | Identify request type and proposal response needs. |
| "Should the firm bid?" | Use bid/no-bid judgement and risk assessment. |
| "The proposal missed pricing/scope/assumptions" | Explain later delivery, governance, and conflict risks. |
| "The client wants the work faster or cheaper" | Use negotiation, scope, assumptions, quality, and delivery consequences. |
| "The client wants extra work if there is time" | Treat as scope and change-control issue, not informal goodwill. |
| "What should be in the proposal?" | Use the proposal structure and apply it to the scenario. |

### What a Strong Commit Answer Includes

- the type of client request or engagement decision;
- what the proposal or agreement must clarify;
- the risk of vague scope, assumptions, budget, timeline, or responsibility;
- how negotiation should preserve both client value and deliverability;
- what should be documented before work begins;
- a clear recommendation about bid/no-bid, proposal content, contract clarification, or change-control setup.

### Common Commit Mistakes

- Treating a proposal as only a persuasive sales pitch.
- Forgetting assumptions, exclusions, and client responsibilities.
- Saying "negotiate a win-win" without naming scope, price, time, quality, or bottom line.
- Ignoring capability and profitability in bid/no-bid judgement.
- Assuming a friendly client request does not need documentation.

## Section 3 - Communicate

### What This Section Means

Communicate is about converting consulting work into messages the client can understand, trust, and use. It covers presentations, professional communication, written reports, findings, recommendations, and the difference between information, analysis, and advice.

### What to Study

| Area | Must know |
| --- | --- |
| Professional communication | Audience-aware, structured, concise, evidence-based, respectful, and clear about uncertainty. |
| Presentation purpose | Make the central message clear early, then support it with evidence, reasoning, implications, and next steps. |
| Technical-to-management communication | Translate technical concepts without patronising the audience or losing accuracy. |
| Trust in communication | Tone, listening, responsiveness, evidence, and client language affect credibility and relationship quality. |
| Professional advice | Structured judgement in response to a client problem, not merely an opinion. |
| Information, analysis, advice | Information gives facts; analysis explains meaning; advice recommends what the client should do. |
| Findings and recommendations | Findings describe what was discovered; recommendations explain what should be done about it. |
| Report writing | Reports should answer the client's question, not record a diary of work completed. |
| Executive summary | Decision-focused summary of the main findings, recommendation, and implications. |

### Techniques and Outputs

| Technique/task | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Audience adaptation | Message detail, language, examples, evidence, and medium matched to audience needs. |
| Presentation structure | Problem, findings, implications, recommendation, risks, and next steps. |
| Advice structure | Objective, evidence, options, trade-offs, assumptions, limits, risks, recommendation, and action. |
| Report structure | Table of contents, executive summary, introduction, approach, findings, recommendations, roadmap, appendices. |
| Finding-to-recommendation chain | What was found, why it matters, what should be done, and what risk remains. |
| Communication risk check | Identify possible misunderstanding, resistance, overclaiming, or missing evidence. |

### Exam Triggers

| If the question says... | Think Communicate |
| --- | --- |
| "Explain this technical issue to executives" | Translate without jargon, link to business impact, and show evidence. |
| "The presentation failed" | Diagnose audience, structure, evidence, tone, timing, or unclear message. |
| "The report is too long or reads like a diary" | Focus on client question, executive summary, findings, recommendations, and relevance. |
| "The consultant gave information but no recommendation" | Distinguish information, analysis, and advice. |
| "The client needs advice" | Provide evidence, options, trade-offs, assumptions, recommendation, and next steps. |
| "What is the difference between finding and recommendation?" | Finding is discovered evidence; recommendation is advised action. |

### What a Strong Communicate Answer Includes

- the audience and decision they need to make;
- the message, evidence, and recommendation;
- the difference between facts, analysis, and advice;
- the communication risk if the consultant is vague, technical, patronising, or unstructured;
- the appropriate communication format, such as presentation, report, executive summary, or advice memo;
- a practical next step for improving the communication.

### Common Communicate Mistakes

- Listing facts without making judgement explicit.
- Treating an executive summary as an introduction.
- Writing for the consultant's process instead of the client's decision.
- Using technical detail without linking it to business risk or action.
- Giving recommendations without evidence, assumptions, or trade-offs.
- Adding new material at the end of a report instead of integrating it into the argument.

## Section 4 - Consult

### What This Section Means

Consult is the core investigation and problem-solving work. The consultant gathers evidence, chooses the right engagement format, frames the real problem, uses hypotheses to focus analysis, and moves from ambiguity to a defensible recommendation.

### What to Study

| Area | Must know |
| --- | --- |
| Meetings | Best for governance, decisions, progress, risk, alignment, and shared updates. |
| Interviews | Best for depth, stakeholder perspective, assumptions, hidden issues, and evidence. |
| Workshops | Best for shared understanding, collaboration, prioritisation, design, and problem solving. |
| Evidence trail | Separate observation, stakeholder opinion, consultant interpretation, and follow-up needs. |
| Facilitation | Manage purpose, agenda, participants, timing, power dynamics, outputs, and write-up. |
| Hypothesis-driven consulting | Start with a testable explanation, gather evidence, refine or reject the hypothesis, and avoid defending the first idea blindly. |
| Problem framing | Clarify the real issue, decision required, criteria, constraints, and evidence gap. |
| Structured vs unstructured problems | Structured problems have clear goals and rules; unstructured problems are ambiguous, contested, and judgement-heavy. |
| IESE six-step process | Problem definition, criteria selection, alternative development, analysis, decision, and action plan. |
| Tame vs wicked problems | Tame problems can be controlled through method; wicked problems have no definitive formulation or simple true-false solution. |

### Techniques and Outputs

| Technique/task | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Meeting plan | Purpose, participants, agenda, materials, decision needed, actions, and follow-up. |
| Interview guide | Open, closed, and probing questions linked to evidence needs. |
| Workshop plan | Objective, activities, timing, participant plan, outputs, facilitation approach, and write-up. |
| Hypothesis tree or hypothesis list | Testable working answers and evidence needed to support or reject them. |
| Problem statement | Real issue, affected stakeholders, constraints, evidence gap, and decision need. |
| Option analysis | Alternatives assessed against explicit criteria and trade-offs. |
| Action plan | Steps, owners, timing, resources, dependencies, and follow-up. |

### Exam Triggers

| If the question says... | Think Consult |
| --- | --- |
| "The consultant needs evidence from stakeholders" | Choose meetings, interviews, workshops, or a mix and justify the format. |
| "Stakeholders have different views" | Use interviews or workshops to gather and reconcile evidence. |
| "The problem is unclear" | Define the problem before recommending a solution. |
| "The consultant starts with a possible explanation" | Use hypothesis-driven consulting and evidence testing. |
| "The client wants a recommendation now" | Explain why problem definition, criteria, alternatives, and analysis are needed. |
| "The situation has no obvious correct answer" | Use unstructured problem solving and professional judgement. |
| "Apply a problem-solving framework" | Use the IESE six-step process with scenario facts. |

### What a Strong Consult Answer Includes

- what evidence or decision is needed;
- which consulting format fits the evidence need and why;
- how the consultant will avoid leading questions, vague outputs, or stakeholder domination;
- whether the problem is structured, unstructured, tame, or wicked;
- how hypotheses, criteria, alternatives, and analysis support a defensible recommendation;
- a practical action plan that assigns responsibilities, timing, and follow-up.

### Common Consult Mistakes

- Calling a meeting when an interview or workshop would fit better.
- Saying "engage stakeholders" without explaining what evidence is needed.
- Treating a hypothesis as a fixed answer instead of a testable working explanation.
- Jumping to solutions before defining the real problem and criteria.
- Listing the IESE steps without applying each step to the case.
- Ignoring human, political, organisational, or implementation constraints.

## Section 5 - Control

### What This Section Means

Control is about keeping the engagement deliverable, ethical, and professionally managed when risk, scope, governance, conflict, or stakeholder behaviour threatens the work. It is not about controlling the client; it is about creating visible ownership, decisions, escalation, and evidence-based responses.

### What to Study

| Area | Must know |
| --- | --- |
| Risk | Uncertainty that may affect objectives, including scope, cost, schedule, quality, stakeholder acceptance, legal exposure, technical feasibility, relationship, and reputation. |
| Governance | Decision rights, roles, responsibilities, reporting, escalation, approvals, and sign-off authority. |
| Scope creep | Informal expansion of work without agreed changes to scope, price, timing, resources, or quality. |
| Change control | Assess requested changes for cost, time, quality, resources, deliverables, and agreement impact. |
| Negotiation during delivery | Scope, resources, milestones, responsibilities, change requests, and expectations continue to be negotiated after contracting. |
| Conflict types | Intragroup, intergroup, and client-consultant conflict. |
| Conflict causes | Work overload, deadlines, fear, unclear communication, distrust, loss of control, weak sponsorship, scope ambiguity, and politics. |
| Difficult stakeholders | Difficult behaviour may signal fear, incentives, politics, lack of information, or genuine flaws in the work. |
| Escalation | Use governance when the issue threatens delivery or exceeds the consultant's authority. |

### Techniques and Outputs

| Technique/task | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Risk register | Risk, evidence, consequence, mitigation, owner, and escalation path. |
| Governance map | Decision rights, reporting forums, approval points, issue paths, and sign-off authority. |
| Scope-change assessment | Impact on cost, time, quality, resourcing, deliverables, and contract assumptions. |
| Issue log | Open issue, owner, decision needed, deadline, status, and escalation point. |
| Conflict diagnosis | Type of conflict, causes, stakeholder interests, evidence, and response. |
| Difficult-conversation plan | Purpose, observable facts, desired outcome, likely objections, neutral language, and next action. |

### Exam Triggers

| If the question says... | Think Control |
| --- | --- |
| "The client asks for extra work" | Assess scope, cost, time, quality, resources, and change control. |
| "No one owns the decision" | Use governance, decision rights, and escalation. |
| "The project is at risk" | Identify risk, evidence, consequence, mitigation, owner, and escalation. |
| "Stakeholders are resisting" | Diagnose conflict causes and respond with evidence and neutral language. |
| "The consultant is facing difficult people" | Separate person, behaviour, issue, and decision. |
| "The issue exceeds the consultant's authority" | Escalate through governance, not personal confrontation. |
| "The team or client departments are arguing" | Classify intragroup, intergroup, or client-consultant conflict and justify the label. |

### What a Strong Control Answer Includes

- the specific risk, scope, governance, or conflict issue;
- scenario evidence proving the issue;
- the consequence for client outcome, consultant delivery, relationship, cost, time, or quality;
- mitigation or negotiation response;
- owner, decision maker, approval point, or escalation path;
- communication that is neutral, evidence-based, and professionally bounded.

### Common Control Mistakes

- Defining risk only as "not fixing the client's problem".
- Saying yes to extra work without documenting scope, cost, time, or quality impact.
- Treating governance as generic "management" instead of decision rights and escalation.
- Blaming a difficult stakeholder without diagnosing the cause of resistance.
- Confusing intragroup and intergroup conflict.
- Escalating too early without evidence, or too late after delivery damage has occurred.

## Section 6 - Close

### What This Section Means

Close is about formally completing the engagement and transferring ownership back to the client. It confirms whether deliverables meet scope, what remains unresolved, what support or handover is needed, and how the client and consultant protect the relationship after delivery.

### What to Study

| Area | Must know |
| --- | --- |
| Close-out purpose | Confirm acceptance, complete delivery, document unresolved issues, transfer ownership, and preserve future relationship. |
| Sign-off | Sponsor or authorised decision maker confirms acceptance of deliverables. |
| Quality assurance | Leader review, legal review, risk review, quality checks, and final iterations may occur before completion. |
| Handover | Client receives outputs, ownership, documentation, support arrangements, and next steps. |
| Residual risk | Remaining risks, limitations, dependencies, or unresolved issues are made explicit. |
| Lessons learned | What worked, what did not, and what should improve in future work. |
| Close-out report types | Analytical report, lessons learned report, follow-on action recommendations, ROI report, and end-of-project report. |
| Commercial closure | Acceptance and sign-off can affect invoicing, final payment, and future work. |

### Techniques and Outputs

| Technique/task | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Deliverable acceptance check | Deliverables compared with scope, assumptions, and agreed success conditions. |
| Final presentation | Findings, recommendation, limitations, residual risks, handover, and next steps. |
| Sign-off record | Authorised approval, acceptance conditions, open items, and date. |
| Handover pack | Final report, supporting materials, action plan, owners, support arrangements, and dependencies. |
| Residual-risk list | Remaining risks, consequences, owners, and recommended actions. |
| Lessons learned review | Improvements for client and consulting team. |

### Exam Triggers

| If the question says... | Think Close |
| --- | --- |
| "The engagement is ending" | Check deliverables against scope and prepare formal close-out. |
| "The client disputes whether work is complete" | Use scope, acceptance criteria, sign-off, unresolved issues, and governance. |
| "The client asks what happens next" | Provide handover, residual risks, support, and action plan. |
| "The consultant wants follow-on work" | Close properly first; separate residual recommendations from unagreed new scope. |
| "The final report has unresolved issues" | Document limitations, residual risks, owners, and next steps. |
| "Why does close-out matter?" | Explain acceptance, transfer of ownership, invoicing, lessons learned, and relationship protection. |

### What a Strong Close Answer Includes

- what close-out is confirming;
- how deliverables match the agreed scope and assumptions;
- who signs off and under what authority;
- unresolved issues, residual risks, and follow-on actions;
- handover and support arrangements;
- why formal closure protects client value, consultant accountability, invoicing, and future relationship.

### Common Close Mistakes

- Saying close-out is just "giving the client the solution".
- Forgetting sign-off, acceptance, handover, and residual risks.
- Treating follow-on recommendations as if they were automatically included in the original scope.
- Ignoring quality assurance and final review.
- Ending with a report but no ownership, action plan, or transition.

## Combined Answer Pattern

Use this when a scenario crosses multiple sections.

1. **Locate the engagement point:** Is this a Client, Commit, Communicate, Consult, Control, or Close problem?
2. **Name the concept:** Use the course term, such as proposal, trust, advice, hypothesis, risk, governance, conflict, or close-out.
3. **Use scenario evidence:** Quote or paraphrase the relevant facts from the case.
4. **Explain the consulting implication:** Link the issue to trust, scope, evidence, delivery, risk, decision quality, or relationship.
5. **Recommend the professional action:** Say what the consultant should do next and why.

## High-Value Distinctions

| Distinction | Exam use |
| --- | --- |
| Consultant role vs client decision | Consultants advise, structure, analyse, and support; clients still own decisions and organisational change. |
| Proposal vs contract | Proposal sells and frames the work; contract confirms obligations, responsibilities, and legal terms. |
| Executive summary vs introduction | Executive summary supports a decision; introduction sets context and document structure. |
| Information vs analysis vs advice | Facts are not advice until judgement and recommended action are made clear. |
| Finding vs recommendation | Finding says what was discovered; recommendation says what should be done. |
| Meeting vs interview vs workshop | Meetings align or decide; interviews gather depth; workshops build shared understanding or outputs. |
| Hypothesis vs assumption | Hypothesis is tested and updated; assumption is a stated condition or dependency. |
| Scope change vs informal help | Scope change affects agreement; informal help can create unmanaged scope creep. |
| Risk vs issue | Risk may happen; issue is already happening and needs action. |
| Intragroup vs intergroup conflict | Intragroup is within one group; intergroup is between groups. |
| Delivery completion vs close-out | Delivery provides outputs; close-out confirms acceptance, handover, residual risk, and sign-off. |

## Practice Rotation

Use the 6Cs to stop practice from becoming random.

| Session | Focus | Practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Client + Commit | Review Weeks 1-3, then attempt proposal or engagement life-cycle questions. |
| 2 | Communicate | Review Weeks 4-5 and Week 7 report writing, then write one advice/report answer. |
| 3 | Consult | Review Weeks 8-10, then attempt one evidence-gathering or problem-solving scenario. |
| 4 | Control | Review Week 6 and Week 11, then attempt risk, governance, scope, or conflict questions. |
| 5 | Close | Review Week 11 close-out, then attempt one close-out and residual-risk question. |
| 6 | Mixed | Attempt one full mock exam question and label which 6C section it mainly tests. |
