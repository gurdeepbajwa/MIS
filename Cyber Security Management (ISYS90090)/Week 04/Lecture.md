## Intended Learning Outcomes

- Define the high-level phases of information security risk management.
- Explain how security policy can protect information resources without stopping productive work.
- Explain the role of cybersecurity strategy and the CISO's strategic duties.
- Explain how security education, training, and awareness can improve staff behaviour and security culture.

## Topic Map

`Risk identifies what matters -> strategy makes choices -> policy sets rules -> SETA shapes behaviour -> controls reduce risk -> review checks change`

Week 03 explained threats and attacks. Week 04 asks how managers turn that threat knowledge into coordinated action. The four practices introduced are:

1. Information security risk management.
2. Information security policy.
3. Information security strategy.
4. Security education, training, and awareness (SETA).

These practices do different jobs, but they must support one another. A risk assessment without action does not protect an asset. A policy without staff support may not change behaviour. Training without a clear strategy or policy may send mixed messages. Technology without these practices may address the wrong risk.

## Security Is a Process, Not a Product

No single product can provide security for an organisation. A firewall, lock, alarm, or software package may form one control, but the organisation must also:

- understand its assets and changing threats;
- select controls that fit its risks;
- define rules and responsibilities;
- train and support people;
- test whether controls work;
- respond to incidents; and
- review changes over time.

Security is therefore an ongoing management process. The lecture describes it as assurance that risk and controls remain in a suitable balance. Perfect security is not possible because some risk remains after controls. The aim is controlled risk that supports the organisation's work.

## Information Security Risk Management

Risk management guides the choice of controls. A general standard can list useful controls, but the organisation's actual risk exposure should determine which controls it needs and where it should place them.

### Risk and Risk Exposure

A **risk scenario** exists when a specific threat actor may exploit a specific vulnerability in a specific asset and cause an impact. Risk identification should also state the attack vector: the method or path the actor could use.

The organisation's **risk exposure** is its full set or portfolio of risk scenarios. Managers cannot treat every scenario equally because time, money, people, and attention are limited. They use risk assessment to rank scenarios and direct resources to the most important ones.

### Four Phases

`Identify -> assess -> respond -> review`

| Phase | Main task | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk identification | Name the asset, threat actor, vulnerability, attack vector, and possible impact. | A set of specific risk scenarios. |
| Risk assessment | Estimate likelihood and impact, then rank scenarios by severity. | A prioritised risk list. |
| Risk response | Choose the most suitable and cost-effective response. | Avoid, mitigate, transfer, or accept each risk. |
| Risk review | Monitor changes to assets, threats, vulnerabilities, controls, and the organisation. | Updated scenarios, assessments, and responses. |

Risk management is continuous. A new system, supplier, threat, work practice, or weakness can change the risk profile and require a fresh assessment.

### Likelihood and Impact

- **Likelihood:** How often or how probably the scenario may occur.
- **Impact:** The harm if it occurs, including financial loss, lost work, legal costs, damage to trust, and harm to reputation or business value.

The lecture uses a simple qualitative scale:

- Low = 1
- Medium = 2
- High = 3

`Risk priority = likelihood x impact`

A high-likelihood, high-impact scenario scores 9 and ranks above a low-likelihood, low-impact scenario that scores 1. The exact scale matters less than defining it clearly and applying it consistently across scenarios. This score supports judgement; it does not replace it.

### Risk Appetite and Responses

**Risk appetite** is the amount and type of risk the organisation is willing to accept. Managers may set a threshold and direct treatment resources to scenarios above it.

| Response | Meaning | Key point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avoid | Stop, remove, or redesign the activity that creates the scenario. | Removing an unneeded sensitive asset can remove the related scenario. |
| Mitigate | Add controls that reduce likelihood, impact, or both. | Incident response can reduce impact after an event begins. |
| Transfer | Shift some cost or operational duty to another party. | Outsourcing or insurance may transfer part of the exposure, but the organisation must still manage supplier and business risk. |
| Accept | Make an informed choice to retain the risk. | Acceptance needs an owner, approval, monitoring, and a reason. |

Controls usually leave **residual risk**, which is the risk that remains after treatment.

## Risk-Based Security and Compliance

The lecturer highlighted this comparison as an important possible exam question.

### Compliance Strengths

- Establishes a common minimum level of control.
- Draws on recognised practice and broad expert agreement.
- Helps organisations meet legal, regulatory, contractual, or industry duties.
- Gives managers a starting set of controls and evidence requirements.

### Compliance Limits

- A general checklist may not match the organisation's exact assets, actors, vulnerabilities, and business needs.
- Passing a checklist does not prove that the organisation is secure.
- Managers may treat compliance as the maximum required effort rather than a minimum baseline.
- A static checklist can weaken continuing observation, testing, learning, and review.

### Risk-Assessment Strengths and Limits

A risk assessment tailors controls to the organisation's context and ranks the risks that matter most. However, it depends on complete asset knowledge, sound estimates, skilled judgement, and regular review. An old or incomplete assessment can also create false confidence.

### Strong Management Position

Use both approaches:

1. Use compliance requirements as the minimum baseline.
2. Use risk assessment to identify context-specific gaps and priorities.
3. Add controls that match the organisation's assets, actors, vulnerabilities, and goals.
4. Test the controls and review changes rather than assume that compliance proves security.

## Information Security Policy

A **policy** provides strategic-level guidance from senior management to the people who make decisions and act for the organisation. It states the organisation's position, expectations, and rules.

Policy should support productive work rather than impose controls without considering business needs. Detailed operational steps sit below policy:

`Policy -> standards -> practices and procedures -> guidelines or work instructions`

- **Policy:** High-level direction and rules.
- **Standard:** Required minimum conditions that support policy.
- **Practice or procedure:** Operational guidance on who does a task and how.
- **Guideline or work instruction:** Supporting advice or detailed help.

### Why Policy Matters

Policy:

- tells staff which behaviour the organisation expects;
- states roles, responsibilities, authority, and accountability;
- supports legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal duties;
- provides a basis for enforcement and review; and
- turns senior management's direction into guidance people can apply.

If the organisation has not given staff clear guidance, it may struggle to justify later discipline or prove that it managed the issue responsibly.

### Policy Success Factors

A policy must:

1. Have a clear owner and suitable senior approval.
2. Reach the people whose decisions and behaviour it governs.
3. Use language that the target audience can understand.
4. Be practical, enforceable, and consistent with business work.
5. Appear when a person needs to make the relevant decision.
6. Fit with training, controls, incentives, and the organisation's real culture.
7. Be reviewed and measured against actual behaviour and outcomes.

A long document stored where staff cannot find the relevant rule will have little effect at the point of action.

### Formal Policy and Actual Behaviour

The lecture distinguishes:

- **Formal policy:** The written rules and guidance.
- **Informal policy:** The behaviour that leaders and staff accept in practice.

If a written policy forbids an act but managers ignore the act whenever it occurs, staff learn that the act is accepted. A sound policy program must align the written rule, management action, workplace norms, and enforcement.

To check whether a policy works, compare the desired behaviour with actual decisions and actions. If policy asks staff to do A but staff keep doing B, managers must investigate usability, awareness, incentives, culture, control design, and enforcement.

## Information Security Strategy

An **information security strategy** selects a future course of action that uses formal, informal, and technological controls to reduce risk to confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

The lecture gives three characteristics:

1. **Prescriptive:** It makes choices about future action.
2. **Multi-faceted:** It balances security, resources, risk, business availability, and effectiveness.
3. **Top-down and organisation-wide:** Senior leaders set the direction, which security management turns into joined action across the organisation.

Strategy is influenced by:

- organisational goals and priorities;
- the threat environment;
- laws, contracts, and outside expectations;
- available money, people, and skills;
- current technology and security capability;
- staff culture; and
- senior management support.

### Strategy Compared with Policy

| Strategy | Policy |
| --- | --- |
| Makes choices about direction, priorities, and investment. | States rules, principles, and expected behaviour. |
| Answers: What will we achieve and where will we focus? | Answers: What must or must not people do? |
| Coordinates formal, informal, and technological controls. | Guides decisions and supports consistent action. |

A strategy may choose to isolate a critical intellectual-property system and invest in added controls. Policy then states who may access it, how staff must handle its information, and which actions are prohibited.

## Security Education, Training, and Awareness

**SETA** is an informal control that aims to improve employee security behaviour and build a suitable security culture. It draws its aims from risk assessment, strategy, and policy, and it complements technical controls.

SETA helps staff understand:

- the organisation's security expectations;
- why the rules and controls exist;
- the person's own security duties;
- which behaviour the organisation expects; and
- how to recognise and report problems.

Policy is directive: it states what people may or may not do. SETA guides, explains, reminds, and builds the knowledge and skill needed to apply that policy.

### Why SETA Programs Fail

- The requested behaviour conflicts with workplace culture or management practice.
- Staff receive mixed messages from policy, managers, incentives, and systems.
- The program aims only to record compliance rather than change behaviour.
- Generic modules do not fit the person's role or risks.
- Training competes with other work for attention.
- Risk management, strategy, policy, culture, and SETA do not reinforce one another.

An effective program should fit real work, explain why the behaviour matters, focus on relevant risks, receive management support, and measure behaviour rather than completion alone.

## CISO Leadership

The CISO must connect technical security work with business goals and people across the organisation.

### Main Challenges

1. **Bridge the business-security gap:** Overcome a narrow technical view, communication limits, and shortages of skilled staff.
2. **State the business value of security:** Explain why security work matters despite competing priorities and the false belief that existing controls guarantee safety.

### Four CISO Roles

| Role | Main contribution | Deloitte chart: current -> desired effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategist | Aligns cyber-risk strategy with business goals, investment, and change. | 15% -> 32% |
| Advisor | Works across the organisation to educate, advise, and influence business activity. | 12% -> 35% |
| Technologist | Assesses and implements security technology and technical standards. | 33% -> 12% |
| Guardian | Protects assets by understanding threats and managing the security program. | 41% -> 22% |

The chart's main point is the desired shift from a mainly technical and protective role towards more strategy and advice. The CISO still needs all four roles, but the right mix depends on the organisation.

## How the Four Practices Work Together

| Practice | Main question | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk management | What could harm us, and what matters most? | Identifies, ranks, treats, and reviews risk. |
| Strategy | What direction and choices will we take? | Aligns priorities, resources, and controls with business goals. |
| Policy | Which rules and responsibilities apply? | Guides decisions and provides a basis for consistent action. |
| SETA | How will people understand and apply the desired behaviour? | Builds knowledge, skill, awareness, and culture. |

Technological controls support these practices but cannot replace them.

## Exam and Quiz Use

### High-Priority Recall

- Four risk phases: **identify, assess, respond, review**.
- Four responses: **avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept**.
- Assessment: **likelihood x impact**, applied consistently.
- Compliance gives a baseline; risk assessment provides context and priority.
- Strategy makes choices; policy states rules; SETA supports behaviour.
- Policy must have an owner, reach its audience, make sense, be usable, be enforceable, and fit actual culture.
- Four CISO roles: **strategist, advisor, technologist, guardian**.

### Applied Answer Structure

> The organisation should first identify the asset, actor, vulnerability, vector, and impact. It should assess likelihood and impact, choose an avoid, mitigate, transfer, or accept response, and review changes. Compliance requirements should form a minimum baseline, while the risk assessment should tailor further controls to the organisation. Strategy should set the direction, policy should state clear rules and responsibilities, and SETA should help staff apply them in practice.

### Common Errors

- Treating compliance as proof that the organisation is secure.
- Using a generic control list without linking it to a risk scenario.
- Confusing likelihood with impact.
- Treating risk acceptance as ignoring a risk.
- Assuming outsourcing removes all responsibility.
- Confusing strategy choices with policy rules.
- Measuring SETA only through module completion.
- Assuming the CISO is only a technical manager.
