# Week 08 — Security Education, Training and Awareness (SETA)

Course: [[Cyber Security Management (ISYS90090)/Course Overview|Cyber Security Management]]

Sources: Week 8 slides and lecture transcript ([[Cyber Security Management (ISYS90090)/Assets/Week 08-10 - Canvas Source Index|source index]]), plus Desman (2003) from Canvas section C.1. This is a quick-study distillation, not a full lecture record.

## Start-to-Finish Map

**Problem:** people are the weakest point, and social engineering exploits that → **Control:** SETA, the *informal* control that tries to change behaviour → **Design:** separate education, training and awareness, then run a seven-step program → **Barrier:** organisational culture → **Test:** is the behaviour still happening a year later?

SETA is not a special case. It sits in the same triplet logic as policy and technical controls: it exists to mitigate a risk to an asset, so it should target identified risk scenarios.

> Plain version: policy says what people must do; SETA is how you get them to actually do it.

## 1. Education vs Training vs Awareness

Learn this table. The three are different tools, and exam answers lose marks when they are folded together.

| | Education | Training | Awareness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question answered | **Why** | **How** | **What** |
| Level | Insight | Knowledge | Information |
| Objective | Understanding | Skill | Exposure |
| Teaching | Theoretical instruction | Practical instruction | Media |
| Methods | Seminars, background reading | Lectures, case workshops, hands-on practice | Videos, newsletters, posters |
| Test measure | Essay (interpret) | Problem solving (apply) | True/false, multiple choice (identify) |
| Impact | Long term | Intermediate | Short term |
| Aimed at | Security leaders who design programs | Everyday users who need skills | Everyone, repeatedly |

Source for table: Guttman and Roback (1995), NIST SP 800-12, as presented in the slides.

**Intuition:** awareness is "remember security exists", training is "here is how to run the antivirus", education is "here is the theory so you can design the program". The lecturer's point on theory: a good theory (such as the triplet model) compresses thousands of cases, so leaders do not need to live through each one.

## 2. What a Good Program Looks Like

Four design features, each with a cost:

- **Current:** uses up-to-date learning methods.
- **Situated:** delivered inside the workplace. Taking people out for training leads to "training tourism": they return and the organisation's inertia overrides what they learned.
- **Small:** workshops for small, relevant groups, not broad-spectrum sessions.
- **Sustained:** repeated over time, not one burst.

A program meeting all four is not the cheap option.

## 3. Seven Steps of a SETA Program

If an exam says "design a SETA program", show these in order, then evaluation and culture.

1. Identify program **scope, goals and objectives**
2. Identify **training staff**
3. Identify **target audiences**
4. **Motivate** management and employees
5. **Administer** the program
6. **Maintain** the program
7. **Evaluate** the program

Key emphasis from the lecture:

- **Step 1 is where programs fail.** Study the current culture first: what behaviour exists, why, and what sustains it. Respect it. People usually have a reason, and enforcement alone can damage productivity.
- **Step 4 means "what's in it for them?"** Saying it is good for the organisation is not enough, and a security leader often lacks power over executives.
- **Steps 6–7 mean the program must adapt.** It will not work first time.

### Extra Materials — Example of "find the reason first"

The lecturer described a Malaysian PhD study of employees posting sensitive work information on Facebook. Managers blamed disrespect for policy. Employees said supervisors answered social media tags but not email, and deadlines left no time to wait. Tougher penalties would have missed the cause. This example is from the lecture transcript.

## 4. Who Trains, and Who Is Trained

**Trainers:** in-house specialists (large organisations), internal ad hoc non-specialists (cheap, uneven quality) or external providers (costly, rarely tailored). Most organisations mix them.

**Tailoring** has two dimensions: functional background (general, managerial, technical user) and skill level (novice, intermediate, advanced). Watch for people who *perceive* themselves as experts, especially executives who set culture.

## 5. Delivery Methods and Awareness Tools

| Method | Strength | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One-on-one | Personal, customised | Expensive |
| Formal class | Community learning, efficient | Not responsive; hard to schedule |
| Computer-based | Flexible, cheap | Customisation limited; often clicked through |
| Distance / web seminar | Low cost | Customisation limited |
| User support group | Informal, interactive | — |
| On-the-job | Applied, inexpensive | Learning curve |
| Self-study | Lowest cost | Shifts quality onto the trainee |

- **Cheap, made in-house:** posters, banners, newsletters, bulletin boards, brochures. **Bought or booked:** videos, computer-based training, lectures, trinkets.
- **Newsletter:** broadcasts policy summaries, news, an events calendar, announcements and how-tos. It keeps engaged people engaged but does little for disengaged ones.
- **Poster series:** vary and update content, keep it simple but interesting, make the message clear, say how to report violations. A poster left up for a year is ignored.

## 6. Why Programs Stall: Kelman's Behaviour Change Model

Three levels, and the slides stress you cannot skip one.

| Level | Why people follow | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Compliance | Avoid punishment | Works only while watched; needs **certain** and **severe** sanctions |
| 2. Identification | Relationship, wanting to belong to the group | Depends on role models such as a respected team leader |
| 3. Internalisation | Fits their own beliefs and identity | Requires a culture where "this is how we do things" |

Compliance is the cheapest to run and the most superficial, and most corporate SETA stays at this level.

### Extra Materials — Telstra example

Telstra's cyber influence team (set up in 2014; six people, few technical) moved from compliance-only awareness to behaviour change. It built an opt-in champion network chosen on enthusiasm, not seniority, with no pay or promotion. The team asked "what's in it for them?" Word of mouth grew the network from about five members to over seven hundred. Source: Alshaikh and Adamson (2021), as summarised in the lecture.

## 7. Four Challenges to Remember

1. **It competes for attention.** Employees already do a lot of training.
2. **One size does not fit all.** Roles, generations and seniority differ.
3. **Compliance drives the format.** Online modules suit compliance, so people rush through them.
4. **Doing it properly is expensive.** Security is a cost centre, so the effective version rarely fits the budget.

**Culture is the biggest barrier:** "culture eats strategy for breakfast". Culture is what is routine and acceptable, not what the policy says.

## Supplemental: Desman (2003), Ten Commandments of Awareness Training

Short reading from Canvas C.1, kept separate from lecture content. Its ten tips: (1) security is a people issue, not a technical one; (2) speak their language; (3) if they cannot see it, they will not learn it; (4) make your point so you and they can identify it; (5) keep a sense of humour; (6) make your point, support it, conclude it; (7) tell recipients how the requested behaviour affects them; (8) "ride the tame horses"; (9) formalise your training methodology; (10) be timely, even if it means slipping schedules. Note the overlap with the lecture: commandments 1 and 7 match the "people, not technology" and "what's in it for them" points.
